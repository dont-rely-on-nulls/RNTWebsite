A language for changing a schema without breaking what depends on it. A
Kintsugi lens describes a change step by step, and every step runs both
ways: forward, from the old shape to the new one, and back, carrying edits
made in the new shape to the old one. Branches on different versions read
each other through the lens, and merge through it.

#+begin_src kintsugi
lens names-split for S from v1 to v2:
  rename SNAME to NAME
  split NAME on ' ' into FIRST, LAST
  add COUNTRY default 'UK'
  drop STATUS into S_STATUS.
#+end_src

* Why kintsugi
Kintsugi is the craft of mending broken pottery with lacquer and gold. The
break is not hidden; it becomes the most visible part of the bowl, and the
bowl is whole again. A schema change is a break too. Kintsugi does not
pretend it did not happen: it writes down exactly where the break is, and
joins both sides.

* Steps that run both ways
A lens is a list of steps. Forward, it is read from the top; back, from the
bottom, with every step undone.

| Step                       | Forward                                    | Back                          |
|----------------------------+--------------------------------------------+-------------------------------|
| =rename A to B=              | renames the attribute                      | renames it back               |
| =add A default v=            | adds =A=, set to =v=                           | removes =A=                     |
| =drop A into R=              | removes =A=, keeping its values in =R=         | restores =A= from =R=             |
| =split A on s into B, C=     | splits each value at the first =s=           | joins =B=, =s= and =C=              |
| =move A, B to R by K=        | moves attributes into a new relation keyed by =K= | joins them back            |
| =convert A by f, g=          | applies =f=                                  | applies =g=                     |

* Nothing is lost
A lens has to obey two laws. Reading through it and writing back what you
read changes nothing. Writing through it and reading back gives what you
wrote. Every step above keeps both, so every list of steps does too.

That is why =drop= needs =into=: a step that would throw values away is
refused, and the values have to go somewhere you can see. It is the gold in
the seam. =split= cuts at the first separator only, so the rest of a long
name stays in =LAST= and joins back exactly. For =convert=, Sol proves that
=g= undoes =f= before the lens is accepted.

* Branches on different versions
Every relation knows its versions, and every session reads the version its
branch is on. Say =main= is on =v1= and =next= is on =v2=: a session on =next=
sees =S= with =FIRST=, =LAST= and =COUNTRY=, including the tuples that are
still stored the =v1= way, read forward through the lens. A session on =main=
sees =next='s edits backwards, in the shape it expects.

Merging works the same way. Merging =next= into =main= carries each change back
through the lens, so a migration is one merge, not a script, and both
branches stay usable for as long as anyone needs the old shape.

#+begin_src kintsugi
-- once every reader has moved on, keep only the new shape
retire v1 of S.
#+end_src

* In the same runtime
A Forth word written against =v1= keeps working after the change: it opens
=S=, the lens serves it =SNAME=, and what it writes goes back through the same
lens. Nothing in the other subsystems has to know a lens exists.

Lenses are stored as relations, one tuple per step:

#+begin_src sql
-- from pureSQL: every change ever made to S, in order
SELECT LENS, SOURCE, TARGET, STEP FROM LENS WHERE RELATION = 'S' ORDER BY POSITION;
#+end_src
