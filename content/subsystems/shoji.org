Query by example for RNT, and the only subsystem with no syntax to learn.
Shoji opens a relation as an empty skeleton, a window with its heading and
a blank row, and you ask a question by filling the blanks in with what an
answer would look like.

#+begin_src shoji
S  | SNO | SNAME | CITY
   | _s  | P.    | London

SP | SNO | PNO
   | _s  | P2
#+end_src

# more

* Why a shoji
A shoji is a sliding screen: paper panes held in a wooden lattice, a grid
you can almost see through. A skeleton is the same thing. The lattice is the
relation’s heading, the panes are blanks, and whatever you write in a pane
says what you are looking for.

* Skeletons
The example above asks for the names of the suppliers in London that ship
part P2. Nothing in it is a keyword. Each pane holds one of a few kinds of
entry:

| Entry                 | Means                                                  |
|-----------------------+--------------------------------------------------------|
| =London=, =P2=, =300=     | a constant: the row must have this value               |
| =_s=                    | an example element, underlined on screen: any value, but the same one everywhere it appears |
| =>200=, =<>London=      | a condition                                            |
| =P.=                    | print: show this attribute in the answer               |
| =G.=                    | group by this attribute                                |
| =CNT.=, =SUM.=, =AVG.=    | an aggregate over the group                            |

The notation is Moshé Zloof’s, from IBM’s Query-by-Example in the 1970s, and
it has hardly needed to change. An example element is how two skeletons
join: =_s= appears under =SNO= in both windows, so the supplier and the
shipment must agree.

When a condition needs more than one pane, it goes in a condition box:

#+begin_src shoji
SP | SNO | PNO | QTY
   | P.  | _p  | _q

CONDITIONS
_q > 200 and _q < 400
#+end_src

* Changing things
The leftmost pane of a row, under the relation’s name, holds a command for
the whole row: =I.= inserts, =D.= deletes, =U.= updates. A row with a command
and constants is a change; a row with a command and example elements changes
every row the examples match.

#+begin_src shoji
S  | SNO | STATUS
U. | S1  | 30

SP | SNO | PNO
D. | S4  | _p
#+end_src

A skeleton with =I.= and empty panes is a form. Every relation has one, so
people who never write a query can still put data in, and the integrity
rules of every other subsystem still apply to what they enter.

* Your own skeletons
A window can be given a heading of your own. Fill it with =I.= and example
elements from the other windows, and the answers become a new relation:

#+begin_src shoji
S     | SNO | SNAME
      | _s  | _n

SP    | SNO | QTY
      | _s  | >300

HEAVY | SUPPLIER
I.    | _n
#+end_src

=HEAVY= is published under =\relation=, and Shoji keeps the question that
made it, so it can be asked again.

* Skeletons are relations
A saved question is stored the way it looks: one tuple per filled pane, in
=PANE (QUESTION, RELATION, ROW, ATTRIBUTE, ENTRY)=. So the questions people
have asked are themselves something to ask about:

#+begin_src shoji
PANE | QUESTION | ATTRIBUTE
     | P.       | CITY
#+end_src

That is every saved question that looks at a city.

* In the same runtime
Any relation opens as a skeleton, whoever made it. Open =EGG= to see Aleph’s
open promises, open =STACK= and you are looking at a running Forth machine,
open a Dandelion bundle and its answers are rows like any others.

#+begin_src shoji
EGG | PROMISE     | LAID
    | orders-ship | P.
#+end_src

-- from pureSQL: what the HEAVY window found
SELECT SUPPLIER FROM HEAVY;
#+end_src
